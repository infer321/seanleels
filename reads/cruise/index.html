★ Landmark Retina RCT Ophthalmology, Apr 2010

CRUISE: Lucentis for central retinal vein occlusion

Monthly Lucentis (ranibizumab) injections gained about 13 to 15 letters at 6 months in macular edema from central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO), against under 1 letter with sham. Sham patients who started Lucentis after 6 months improved, but did not catch up by 12 months.

Study snapshot

Design
Phase III, randomized 1:1:1, double-masked, sham-controlled, multicenter trial
Population
CRVO macular edema diagnosed within 12 months, best-corrected VA 20/40 to 20/320, OCT central subfield thickness 250 µm or more. An obvious afferent pupillary defect (APD) was an exclusion
N
392 randomized: 130 sham, 132 Lucentis 0.3 mg, 130 Lucentis 0.5 mg
Months 0 to 5
Monthly Lucentis 0.3 mg, Lucentis 0.5 mg, or sham injections
Months 6 to 11
Monthly assessment. Any patient with VA 20/40 or worse, or central subfield thickness 250 µm or more, could receive Lucentis 0.5 mg as needed (PRN). The sham group became eligible for 0.5 mg
Primary outcome
Mean change in best-corrected VA (letters) at month 6
Funding
Not verified

Effect

Both doses beat sham at month 6 (P < .0001 for each). For 0.5 mg the mean gain was 14.9 letters (95% CI 12.6 to 17.2) against 0.8 for sham (95% CI −2.0 to 3.6). Thickness fell fast, and the visual gains held through month 12 with as-needed treatment.

What delay cost

At 12 months the sham group, which began Lucentis 0.5 mg as needed after month 6, gained 7.3 letters against 13.9 for the group treated from the start. Its macular thickness fell to the same level as the treated groups, but vision did not catch up. Waiting 6 months was not harmless.

The comparison is not clean. The early group had 6 monthly injections and then as-needed treatment, while the delayed group had 6 sham injections and then as-needed treatment. It shows that delay cost vision, not whether the same schedule started later would have done as well.

What this was not

It tested benefit against sham, not one anti-VEGF drug against another. Eyes with VA better than 20/40 were not studied, and the APD exclusion means severely ischemic eyes are under-represented, so the result may not apply to them.

How far to trust it

High for the 6-month benefit over sham; moderate for the cost of delay

A large, double-masked, sham-controlled trial with a big and consistent effect on vision and on macular thickness. The delay finding is weaker because it compares two different schedules and followed patients for only 6 months after the sham group crossed over. Fewer sham patients reached the 6-month visit (88.5%, against 97.7% and 91.5% in the two Lucentis groups).

What I'd change

Randomize early monthly, early as-needed and delayed arms on the same schedule, so the cost of delay can be separated from the cost of a lighter regimen. Include eyes with VA better than 20/40 and eyes with an APD, report ischemia on widefield angiography, and run it as a head-to-head against the cheaper off-label Avastin (bevacizumab).

Clinical significance takeaway

For vision-impairing macular edema from CRVO, prompt anti-VEGF treatment gives meaningful visual gains, and treatment usually means repeated injections. A CRUISE-supported option is Lucentis 0.5 mg every 4 weeks, with VA and OCT reassessment each visit. I would not wait for spontaneous improvement. The 6-month loading period does not mean treatment stops after 6 injections: CRUISE moved to as-needed treatment with monthly review, and HORIZON found vision fell when follow-up was stretched in year 2. Current AAO guidance treats anti-VEGF as the first-line approach for macular edema from retinal vein occlusion.

Bottom line

CRUISE 2010: for macular edema from CRVO with VA 20/40 to 20/320, monthly Lucentis (ranibizumab) 0.5 mg beat sham at 6 months (+14.9 vs +0.8 letters); start promptly and keep monthly review, since a 6-month delay never fully caught up.
