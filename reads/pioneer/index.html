PIONEER: optogenetic therapy for blindness from retinitis pigmentosa

Encouraging early evidence that gene therapy plus light-stimulating goggles may give limited visual function to some profoundly blind patients with RP, from a 10-person safety study that cannot establish efficacy.

Study snapshot

Design
Multicenter, phase 1, open-label, nonrandomized, dose-escalation study
Population
Adults with blindness from advanced retinitis pigmentosa
N
10 participants
Intervention
GS030: one intravitreal injection of an AAV vector carrying ChrimsonR into the worse-seeing eye, at three dose levels, plus light-stimulating goggles
Comparator
None (single arm)
Primary outcome
Safety. Light sensitivity (full-field stimulus threshold) secondary; behavioral tasks and EEG exploratory
Follow-up
Up to 5 years; 4 participants completed 5 years
Funding
GenSight Biologics, the developer; one author is a GenSight employee
Registration
NCT03326336

Read with

  • Earlier case reportSahel et al., Nature Medicine 2021One blind patient treated with the same therapy, who located and counted objects with the goggles.

What they found

Treatment and mechanism

GS030 has two components: intravitreal gene therapy and light-stimulating goggles. An AAV vector delivers ChrimsonR, a light-sensitive ion channel, to surviving retinal ganglion cells. The goggles convert visual information into amber-light patterns that activate those cells.

This aims to bypass lost photoreceptor function. It does not correct the RP mutation, replace photoreceptors, or establish improved cell survival.

Safety

34 ocular adverse events occurred among 9 participants: 23 mild, 10 moderate, one severe. The severe event was a transient central retinal artery occlusion immediately after injection, resolving within minutes after apraclonidine (Iopidine). Inflammation occurred in 7 of 10.

No systemic adverse event was attributed to the vector or injection. Three mild events were attributed to the goggles. These observations characterize safety in a small cohort; they do not establish safety conclusively.

Light sensitivity

The full-field stimulus threshold measured the minimum detectable light intensity using the goggles, with the untreated eye patched. Lower threshold means better sensitivity. 7 of 10 improved, ranging from 2.0-fold to 62.3-fold, and 6 of 10 crossed the ≥0.6-log-unit threshold, approximately fourfold greater sensitivity.

That "clinically meaningful" threshold was not prespecified. Improved light detection does not automatically mean improved object recognition or detailed vision.

Functional vision and EEG

Tasks included detecting, locating, and touching objects, plus identifying bar orientation. Four of eight tested participants were classified as behavioral responders. This responder definition was also not prespecified. The results suggest possible benefit in a subset, without establishing efficacy.

EEG was a separate assessment of whether cortical activity distinguished visual stimuli, in five selected participants. The group estimate was imprecise: a 7.6-percentage-point improvement in decoding accuracy (95% CI −0.1 to 15.4).

How far to trust it

Very low for visual-function benefit

Single-arm and open-label, with 10 participants, and the thresholds and responder definitions were set after the fact.

Encouraging early evidence that the system may provide limited visual function in some profoundly impaired patients. But there was no randomized or sham control, only 10 participants, variable follow-up, and incomplete behavioral testing. No high-acuity vision restoration was demonstrated.

The number that matters

4of8
Behavioral responders

Participants who completed behavioral testing and were classified as responders on detecting, locating or touching objects. The responder definition was not prespecified.

What I'd change

Add a randomized delayed-treatment arm and give every participant the same standardized goggle training, with functional outcomes and responder definitions prespecified.

Clinical significance takeaway

If a patient with end-stage RP asked me about this, I would say it remains investigational. So far the best case is that some people regain goggle-dependent detection and localization of high-contrast objects, not reading or face recognition. I would point them toward enrolling trials and low-vision rehabilitation now rather than waiting.

Bottom line

Optogenetic therapy may give limited, goggle-dependent visual function to some blind RP patients, but it needs controlled trials with prespecified outcomes before it can be called effective.
