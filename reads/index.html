Landmark trials

Papers that changed guidelines or standard practice.

  1. Nov 2026

    CATT and IVAN: bevacizumab vs ranibizumab for neovascular AMD

    NEJM, 2011 · Lancet, 2013 Retina RCT Read with IVAN's 1-year results
    High certainty, vision on the same schedule

    Per CATT (2011), for treatment-naive neovascular AMD, intravitreal bevacizumab 1.25 mg is equivalent to ranibizumab 0.5 mg for 1-year vision on the same monthly schedule (8.0 vs 8.5 letters), and IVAN (2013) found a similar but inconclusive difference, so I'd start bevacizumab for this patient with monthly visits, because it cost about $50 a dose vs $2,000 in CATT and as-needed dosing still needs monthly monitoring.

  2. Nov 2026

    OHTS: treating ocular hypertension to prevent glaucoma

    Arch Ophthalmol, 2002 Glaucoma RCT Read with the risk-factor analysis and 20-year follow-up
    High certainty, 5-year result

    Per OHTS (2002), for ocular hypertension, topical drops lowering IOP by at least 20% halved 5-year conversion to glaucoma (4.4% vs 9.5%; NNT about 20), so I'd start latanoprost 0.005% nightly for this patient if their baseline risk is moderate or high (older age, higher IOP, thinner cornea, larger cup-to-disc ratio), and observe if it's low.

  3. Nov 2026

    Protocol T: aflibercept, bevacizumab or ranibizumab for diabetic macular edema

    NEJM, 2015 Retina RCT Read with the 2-year results and Protocol AC
    High certainty, 1-year result

    Per Protocol T (2015), for center-involved DME with vision 20/50 or worse, intravitreal aflibercept 2 mg every 4 weeks, then per the retreatment algorithm, is superior to bevacizumab 1.25 mg at 1 year (+6.5 letters), so I'd start aflibercept for this patient; if step therapy is required, Protocol AC (2022) supports starting bevacizumab and switching for a poor response, which reached similar vision by 2 years.

  4. Nov 2026

    EVS: immediate vitrectomy vs tap and inject for endophthalmitis after cataract surgery

    Arch Ophthalmol, 1995 Retina RCT Read with the 2001 diabetes analysis
    Moderate certainty, light-perception result

    Per EVS (1995), for acute post-cataract endophthalmitis with light-perception-only vision, immediate pars plana vitrectomy plus intravitreal vancomycin 1 mg and amikacin 0.4 mg is superior to tap and inject (33% vs 11% reaching 20/40), so I'd plan same-day vitrectomy for this patient; with hand motions or better, immediate vitrectomy was no better than tap and inject, and IV antibiotics added nothing.

  5. Nov 2026

    LiGHT: laser vs eye drops as first treatment for glaucoma and ocular hypertension

    Lancet, 2019 Glaucoma RCT Read with the 6-year follow-up
    High certainty, 3-year result

    Per LiGHT (2019), for newly diagnosed open-angle glaucoma or ocular hypertension, 360° SLT first matched eye drops on quality of life, kept 74% drop-free at 3 years and avoided trabeculectomy (0 vs 11 eyes), so I'd offer SLT first for this patient if they can tolerate it, given the long-term medical and financial burden of daily drops.

  6. Nov 2026

    Protocol S: ranibizumab vs PRP for proliferative diabetic retinopathy

    JAMA, 2015 Retina RCT Read with CLARITY and the 5-year follow-up
    High certainty, 2-year result

    Per Protocol S (2015), for proliferative diabetic retinopathy, intravitreal ranibizumab 0.5 mg every 4 weeks, then per the retreatment algorithm, is noninferior to PRP for vision at 2 years with less new DME (9% vs 28%), so I'd choose anti-VEGF for this patient if they have vision-impairing DME, and PRP if follow-up is uncertain, because the benefit depends on staying in care.

  1. Nov 2026

    PIONEER: optogenetic therapy for blindness from retinitis pigmentosa

    NEJM, 2026 Retina Early-phase trial Read with the 2021 case report
    Very low certainty, visual-function benefit

    Per PIONEER (2026), for blindness from end-stage retinitis pigmentosa, GS030 optogenetic therapy (one intravitreal injection plus light-stimulating goggles) appeared safe in 10 patients, and 4 of 8 tested regained some object detection, but efficacy is unproven, so I'd refer this patient to a clinical trial and low-vision rehabilitation rather than offer it as treatment.
