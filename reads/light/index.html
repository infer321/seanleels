LiGHT: laser vs eye drops as first treatment for glaucoma and ocular hypertension

Laser-first did not improve general quality of life, but it kept 74% of patients off drops for 3 years, and no laser-first eye needed a trabeculectomy.

Study snapshot

Design
Randomized, observer-masked trial, 6 UK hospitals
Population
Newly diagnosed, untreated open-angle glaucoma or ocular hypertension; field loss no worse than −12 dB (better eye) or −15 dB (worse eye)
N
718 patients, 1,235 eyes (555 with glaucoma, 163 with ocular hypertension)
Intervention
360° selective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT), one repeat allowed, then drops if needed
Comparator
Eye drops, escalated by NICE and European Glaucoma Society guidance
Primary outcome
Health-related quality of life (EQ-5D) at 3 years; powered to detect a 0.05 difference
Follow-up
3 years (primary); 6-year results reported in 2023
Funding
National Institute for Health Research (UK)
Registration
ISRCTN32038223

What they found

LiGHT, 3 years

The trial did not show that laser-first improved quality of life: adjusted EQ-5D difference +0.01 (95% CI −0.01 to +0.03). Both treatment pathways achieved good pressure control, with similar average final IOP.

74% of laser-first patients were drop-free at three years, with one repeat SLT permitted. This represents sustained control without daily medication.

Clinical outcomes favored laser-first: progression in 3.8% vs 5.8% of eyes, and 0 vs 11 eyes needing trabeculectomy. These were secondary, exploratory outcomes, so I would avoid claiming definitive superiority for preventing progression.

Overall serious adverse events were similar, but patients in the drops arm reported more drop-related side effects (150 events in 73 patients vs 30 in 20). SLT commonly caused transient symptoms (34% of patients); pressure rose more than 5 mmHg on the day of treatment in 6 eyes, and only 1 needed treatment.

Laser-first saved approximately £458 per patient in ophthalmology costs over three years. Once community and non-eye costs were added, the saving was no longer significant. Laser patients also had more clinic visits (3,441 vs 2,907), because of a protocol 2-week pressure check that never changed management. The economic advantage was demonstrated within the UK healthcare setting.

LiGHT, 6 years · Ophthalmology 2023

Progression in 19.6% vs 26.8% of eyes (P = .006), trabeculectomy in 13 vs 32 eyes, and cataract surgery in 57 vs 95 eyes. 69.8% of laser-first eyes stayed at target with no drops or surgery. After year 3, drops-arm patients could switch to SLT, which would shrink the difference, not inflate it.

What changed

NICE updated its glaucoma guideline in 2022 to recommend 360° SLT as first-line treatment for newly diagnosed ocular hypertension and open-angle glaucoma, citing LiGHT.

How far to trust it

High for the 3-year result

Strengths: randomized allocation, masked clinical measurements, standardized treatment-escalation rules, and approximately 91% primary-questionnaire completion.

Risk of bias, LiGHT (3-year result)
DomainConcernWhy
RandomizationLowWeb-based, stratified by diagnosis and centre
Deviations from intended treatmentLowEscalation set by decision-support software, not clinician choice
Missing dataLow91% returned the 3-year questionnaire; sensitivity analyses agreed
MeasurementLowPressure, fields and disc imaging measured by masked observers
Selective reportingLowRegistered (ISRCTN32038223); the prespecified primary outcome was reported, though null

Primary-outcome limitation: EQ-5D measures general health and may miss glaucoma-specific benefits. Baseline scores were already high, leaving limited room for improvement. The funder required EQ-5D for a NICE cost-utility analysis, so it was chosen for health economics, not for glaucoma.

Patients and treating clinicians were unmasked, which could influence reported symptoms and quality of life. This was deliberate: drop-freedom is the benefit, and placebo drops would have hidden it.

Some missing three-year questionnaire values were replaced with 30-month values; sensitivity analyses agreed.

The population consisted of newly diagnosed, untreated patients with ocular hypertension or early-to-moderate glaucoma (77% had glaucoma). I would be cautious about applying the findings to advanced disease or patients outside the eligibility criteria.

Three years provides useful medium-term evidence, but does not establish lifelong drop freedom or long-term prevention of blindness.

What I'd change

Use a glaucoma-specific primary outcome instead of EQ-5D.

Clinical significance takeaway

I would consider SLT as an initial option for eligible patients, including those who could tolerate drops. SLT may be particularly useful when obtaining or consistently administering daily medication is difficult. Laser removes the need for daily drops, not the need for follow-up: both approaches still require monitoring, and SLT may wear off or require retreatment.

Financial barriers could make reduced medication dependence valuable, but this trial did not establish a specific benefit for low-income patients or lower US out-of-pocket costs. Drops remain reasonable depending on patient preference, contraindications, response, and tolerability.

Bottom line

LiGHT 2019, for new OAG or ocular hypertension, 360° SLT first matches drops on quality of life, keeps 74% drop-free at 3 years and avoids trabeculectomy (0 vs 11 eyes); offer first if tolerated, sparing years of daily drops and their cost.
