CATT and IVAN: bevacizumab vs ranibizumab for neovascular AMD

Given on the same schedule, off-label bevacizumab matched ranibizumab for vision in CATT. IVAN found a similar small difference, but with a stricter margin it could not confirm noninferiority on its own.

Study snapshot

Design
Both: multicenter 2×2 randomized noninferiority trials, drug (bevacizumab vs ranibizumab) by schedule (monthly vs as needed)
Population
Both: age 50 or older, previously untreated neovascular AMD. CATT: vision 20/25 to 20/320, 44 US centers. IVAN: at least 25 letters, 23 UK hospitals
N
CATT: 1208 randomized, 1185 analyzed. IVAN: 628 randomized, 610 treated and analyzed
Doses
Bevacizumab 1.25 mg or ranibizumab 0.5 mg, intravitreal
Primary outcome
CATT: mean change in vision at 1 year. IVAN: vision at 2 years
Funding
CATT: National Eye Institute. IVAN: UK NIHR Health Technology Assessment programme
Registration
CATT: NCT00593450. IVAN: ISRCTN92166560

What they found

Both trials asked the same two questions: is cheap, off-label bevacizumab as good as licensed ranibizumab, and can injections be given less often than monthly?

CATT, 1 year

On matching schedules, the drugs were equivalent: monthly bevacizumab gained 8.0 letters vs 8.5 with monthly ranibizumab, and as needed 5.9 vs 6.8. Ranibizumab was numerically better, but the main finding was that bevacizumab met the trial's margin.

Ranibizumab needed fewer injections only in the as-needed groups (6.9 vs 7.7 of a possible 13). Monthly groups got monthly injections by design.

Serious systemic adverse events, mostly hospitalizations, were more common with bevacizumab (24.1% vs 19.0%; risk ratio 1.29, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.66). This was a broad signal spread across many kinds of events. Deaths, heart attacks and strokes were similar between drugs.

IVAN, 2 years

Bevacizumab minus ranibizumab was −1.37 letters (95% CI −3.75 to +1.01). Bevacizumab could be slightly better, or up to 3.75 letters worse, and IVAN allowed only 3.5 letters worse. So IVAN alone was inconclusive: it neither showed inferiority nor established noninferiority. A nonsignificant P value doesn't settle that.

Intermittent vs monthly treatment was also inconclusive under the prespecified margin (−1.63 letters, 95% CI −4.01 to 0.75).

Together

  • CATT: similar one-year vision between drugs on matching schedules; bevacizumab met noninferiority.
  • IVAN: small estimated differences, but confidence intervals crossed its stricter noninferiority boundary.
  • As-needed dosing reduced injections, not monitoring: both trials still saw patients every month.
  • CATT's systemic safety signal warrants attention, but it doesn't prove bevacizumab causes more vascular events.

How far to trust it

High for equal vision on the same schedule

Two large randomized trials with masked vision examiners point the same way. The certainty applies to vision, not to the systemic safety difference, which neither trial was sized to settle.

Letters gained at 1 year in CATT. In IVAN the drug difference was −1.37 letters (95% CI −3.75 to +1.01), crossing its −3.5 margin.

What I'd change

Power the trial for systemic safety as well as vision: prespecify which serious adverse events count and size it to detect a difference like CATT's 24.1% vs 19.0%, so the safety question isn't left to an unexpected signal.

Clinical significance takeaway

These trials support bevacizumab as an effective alternative when choosing between these drugs, while reinforcing that intermittent injections still require reliable monitoring.

Bottom line

CATT 2011, for treatment-naive wet AMD, bevacizumab 1.25 mg q4wk is equivalent to ranibizumab 0.5 mg for 1-year vision (8.0 vs 8.5 letters; IVAN 2013 similar but inconclusive); first line at ~$50 vs $2,000 a dose, with monthly visits even if dosing PRN.
