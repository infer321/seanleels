AREDS2: lutein, zeaxanthin and omega-3 for AMD

Adding lutein/zeaxanthin or omega-3 to the AREDS supplements did not further reduce progression to advanced AMD in the primary analysis. Swapping lutein/zeaxanthin in for beta-carotene looked as good or better, and avoids a lung cancer signal.

Study snapshot

Design
Multicenter, randomized, double-masked, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial, 2×2 factorial, with a second optional randomization of the AREDS formulation itself
Population
Ages 50 to 85 at high risk of advanced AMD: large drusen in both eyes, or large drusen in one eye and advanced AMD in the other. Eligible only after taking at least 75% of run-in medications
N
4,203 participants; 3,036 (72%) joined the second randomization
Interventions
Lutein 10 mg + zeaxanthin 2 mg; DHA 350 mg + EPA 650 mg; both; or placebo. Second randomization: AREDS formulation with or without beta-carotene (15 mg), and zinc 80 mg or 25 mg
Comparator
"Placebo" means no added lutein/zeaxanthin or omega-3. Participants still took the AREDS supplements
Primary outcome
Progression to advanced AMD (neovascular AMD or central geographic atrophy), analyzed by eye
Follow-up
Median 5 years
Funding
National Institutes of Health
Registration
NCT00345176

Read with

  • Original trialAREDS report no. 8, Arch Ophthalmol 2001Antioxidants plus zinc reduced progression to advanced AMD (OR 0.72, 99% CI 0.52 to 0.98), the formula AREDS2 built on.
  • Secondary analysesAREDS2 report no. 3, JAMA Ophthalmol 2014Lutein/zeaxanthin vs beta-carotene, and by baseline AMD status.

What they found

AREDS2 results, progression to advanced AMD
ComparisonFinding
Add lutein/zeaxanthinNo significant benefit: 29% vs 31% at 5 years; HR 0.90 (98.7% CI 0.76 to 1.07), P = 0.12
Add omega-3No significant benefit: 31% vs 31%; HR 0.97 (98.7% CI 0.82 to 1.16), P = 0.70
Add bothNo significant benefit: 30% vs 31%; HR 0.89 (98.7% CI 0.75 to 1.06), P = 0.10
Lutein/zeaxanthin in place of beta-caroteneExploratory benefit: HR 0.82 (95% CI 0.69 to 0.96), P = 0.02
Remove beta-carotene, or lower zinc from 80 to 25 mgNo apparent effect on progression

The replacement result was an exploratory subgroup comparison, with no adjustment for multiple comparisons, so it is supportive evidence with less certainty than a positive primary result. A prespecified analysis pooling everyone who took lutein/zeaxanthin against everyone who didn't was also modestly favorable (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.82 to 0.99, P = 0.04).

Safety

Lung cancer was more frequent with beta-carotene than without (23 cases, 2.0%, vs 11 cases, 0.9%; nominal P = 0.04), mostly in former smokers. Current smokers and people who had quit within the past year were randomized only to formulas without beta-carotene, yet the signal appeared in former smokers, so ask about past smoking, not just current smoking. Lung cancer rates were the same with and without lutein/zeaxanthin (1.5% in both).

How far to trust it

High for no added benefit from omega-3 or added lutein/zeaxanthin

A large, double-masked trial with central masked grading of photographs. The rating applies to the primary result only. The benefit of lutein/zeaxanthin as a substitute for beta-carotene comes from exploratory analyses, so I'd call that low to moderate.

Risk of bias, primary progression outcome
DomainConcernWhy
RandomizationLowRandomized allocation; baseline groups were comparable
Deviations from intended treatmentLowDouble-masked supplements, intention-to-treat analysis
Missing dataSome concernsParticipants lost to follow-up or who died were censored at last contact, which assumes the remaining follow-up is informative; I didn't verify the exact numbers
MeasurementLowStandardized photographs graded centrally by masked graders
Selective reportingLow for the primary resultThe primary comparisons were reported, including the null findings

These are provisional judgments from the published methods, not a completed formal RoB 2 assessment. The main interpretive risk is leaning on favorable exploratory analyses. Separately, the authors note that participants were well-nourished, with above-average dietary intake, so the results may not generalize to people with poor diets. That limits external validity rather than showing bias within the trial.

The number that matters

Progression to advanced AMD vs placebo (98.7% CI 0.76 to 1.07). No significant additional benefit.

What I'd change

Prespecify lutein/zeaxanthin vs beta-carotene as a primary comparison, so the question clinicians actually need answered isn't left to an exploratory analysis.

Clinical significance takeaway

For intermediate AMD in both eyes, or intermediate AMD with advanced AMD in the fellow eye, I'd recommend daily AREDS2 supplementation. I'd check current and former smoking history and use a beta-carotene-free formula.

Total daily formula: vitamin C 500 mg, vitamin E 400 IU, lutein 10 mg, zeaxanthin 2 mg, zinc 80 mg, copper 2 mg. Pill count depends on the product. The aim is to reduce progression, not restore lost vision.

Bottom line

AREDS2 2013: for intermediate AMD, adding omega-3 or extra lutein/zeaxanthin to AREDS doesn't further cut progression (HR 0.90, not significant); use a beta-carotene-free formula with lutein/zeaxanthin instead, especially with any smoking history.
