ETDRS: focal laser and aspirin in diabetic eye disease

ETDRS showed that focal laser to clinically significant macular edema reduces visual loss, and that aspirin neither treats diabetic retinopathy nor raises hemorrhage risk. Laser prevented worsening more than it restored vision, and anti-VEGF has since replaced it as first line for center-involving DME.

Study snapshot

Design
Multicenter randomized trial in diabetic retinopathy with two separate randomizations: laser timing, and aspirin vs placebo
Laser question (Report 1)
754 eyes with macular edema and mild to moderate retinopathy assigned to immediate focal argon laser; 1,490 eyes assigned to deferral
Aspirin question
3,711 patients with mild to severe nonproliferative or early proliferative retinopathy, aspirin 650 mg/day vs placebo
Key definition
Clinically significant macular edema (CSME): thickening that involves or threatens the center of the macula, judged by stereo biomicroscopy or stereo photographs, even if vision is not yet reduced
Funding
Supported by the National Eye Institute

Read with

  • Same trialETDRS Reports 8 and 20 (1991, 1995)Aspirin did not change retinopathy, and did not increase vitreous hemorrhage in severity or duration.
  • Same trialETDRS Report 14 (JAMA 1992)Aspirin effects on mortality and cardiovascular events in the same patients.
  • Later trialDRCR Protocol V (JAMA 2019)Observation was reasonable for center-involving DME with good vision. See the Protocol V appraisal.
  • Later trialDRCR Protocol T (NEJM 2015)Anti-VEGF drug comparison for center-involving DME. See the Protocol T appraisal.

Effect

Focal laser to eyes with CSME reduced the risk of visual loss and increased the chance of improvement. Its purpose was to prevent worsening, not to restore normal vision. This was macular laser, not the peripheral panretinal laser used for proliferative disease.

Aspirin

Aspirin 650 mg/day did not prevent high-risk proliferative retinopathy or reduce visual loss, and it did not increase vitreous hemorrhage in occurrence, severity or duration. The authors concluded there were no ocular contraindications to aspirin when it is needed for cardiovascular or other medical reasons. That supports continuing aspirin, not prescribing it for retinopathy.

What this was not

CSME was defined clinically, not by OCT, so it is not identical to modern center-involving DME. The trial predates anti-VEGF, so it does not show that laser beats today's first-line treatment. It does not apply to proliferative disease treated with panretinal laser.

How far to trust it

Moderatefor the laser and aspirin findings; original methods not verified

A large randomized trial with objective vision outcomes and a clear, durable message. I could not check the original methods, and the laser findings come from an era before OCT and anti-VEGF.

What I'd change

Define macular edema with OCT, report patient-level outcomes alongside eye-level ones, and publish prespecified laser outcomes with absolute risks in the abstract. Today the key comparison is laser against anti-VEGF, which DRCR trials have since addressed.

Clinical significance takeaway

Classify DME with OCT and visual acuity. For center-involving DME with vision loss, anti-VEGF is first line. For good vision, observation with reliable follow-up can be reasonable. Focal or grid laser remains an option for non-center-involving edema. Do not stop aspirin solely because of diabetic retinopathy.

Bottom line

ETDRS 1985: for eyes with clinically significant diabetic macular edema, focal laser reduced visual loss versus deferral; use OCT and vision to choose today (anti-VEGF if center-involving with vision loss), and do not stop medically indicated aspirin for retinopathy alone.
