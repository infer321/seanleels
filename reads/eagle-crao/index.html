EAGLE: intra-arterial thrombolysis for CRAO

Local intra-arterial rtPA did not improve vision more than conservative treatment in acute central retinal artery occlusion, and caused more adverse reactions. Treatment was given late, at about 11 to 13 hours.

Study snapshot

Design
Prospective, randomized, multicenter superiority trial
Population
Adults aged 18 to 75 with acute nonarteritic central retinal artery occlusion, symptoms for 20 hours or less, at 9 centers in Germany and Austria
N
84 randomized (44 intra-arterial, 40 conservative); 82 analyzed. The design called for 200
Intervention
Local intra-arterial fibrinolysis: catheter-directed rtPA into the ophthalmic artery, up to 50 mg, followed by heparin for 5 days
Comparator
Conservative standard treatment: ocular massage, topical beta-blocker and acetazolamide to lower IOP, aspirin, heparin, and hemodilution depending on hematocrit
Primary outcome
Best-corrected visual acuity at 1 month. Safety was secondary
Follow-up
1 month
Funding
Not verified

No added benefit, more harm

Vision improved significantly in both groups (both P < 0.0001) and did not differ between them (P = 0.69). The data and safety monitoring committee recommended stopping after the first interim analysis because efficacy looked similar and adverse reactions were more common with intra-arterial treatment. The authors could not recommend intra-arterial fibrinolysis for acute CRAO. The adverse reaction percentages cover all reported reactions; they are not an intracranial hemorrhage rate.

What this trial tested

This was intra-arterial rtPA, not intravenous alteplase. The conservative regimen is historical, and its inclusion does not establish that it works. Vision improved in both groups, but with no untreated comparison, that improvement can't be credited to either treatment.

The critical limitation: treatment was late

Treatment started a mean of 12.8 hours after symptoms in the intra-arterial group and 11.0 hours in the conservative group. EAGLE doesn't support routinely offering its catheter-directed regimen, but it can't settle whether much earlier treatment, particularly by a different route, helps selected patients.

How far to trust it

Low beyond this delayed-treatment intra-arterial protocol

A randomized trial, but small, stopped early at 84 of a planned 200, with 1-month follow-up and late treatment. Failing to find a difference does not show the treatments are equivalent. The signal of more adverse reactions with intra-arterial treatment is clearer than the vision result.

What I'd change

Enroll patients within a few hours of onset, compare intravenous and intra-arterial routes against a standard-care arm, and mask the visual acuity assessment, so the question of early reperfusion can be answered without the delay that limited EAGLE.

Clinical significance takeaway

EAGLE argues against routine intra-arterial rtPA using this delayed-treatment protocol. Acute CRAO still needs immediate stroke evaluation. I'd treat it as a retinal stroke: document last known well, arrange urgent stroke-center evaluation with ophthalmology involved, screen for giant cell arteritis, and let the stroke team assess any time-sensitive reperfusion options. I wouldn't delay transfer for ocular massage or paracentesis, which have no established benefit. Vascular secondary prevention and ophthalmic follow-up for complications follow.

Bottom line

EAGLE 2010: for acute nonarteritic CRAO within 20 hours, intra-arterial rtPA was no better than conservative care for vision (57.1% vs 60.0% improved ≥0.3 logMAR) and had more adverse reactions (37.1% vs 4.3%); don't offer it routinely, and send for immediate stroke-center evaluation instead.
