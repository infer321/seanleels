EVS: immediate vitrectomy vs tap and inject for endophthalmitis after cataract surgery

Immediate vitrectomy helped only eyes presenting with light perception vision; for everyone else, tap and inject was enough, and the tested IV antibiotics added nothing.

Study snapshot

Design
Randomized 2 × 2 factorial trial, Endophthalmitis Vitrectomy Study Group, 27 US centers
Population
Bacterial endophthalmitis within 6 weeks of cataract surgery or secondary IOL implantation; vision from light perception to worse than about 20/50; no light perception excluded
N

What they found

EVS, 1995

Multicenter randomized trial of 420 patients with bacterial endophthalmitis developing within six weeks of cataract surgery or secondary IOL implantation. 2 × 2 factorial design: immediate PPV versus initial vitreous tap/biopsy, each with or without systemic antibiotics. Eligible presenting vision ranged from light perception to approximately 20/50. Patients with no light perception were excluded.

Everyone received intravitreal vancomycin and amikacin. The systemic-antibiotic groups additionally received ceftazidime plus amikacin, generally for 5–10 days. Initial treatment began within six hours. Patients in either group could undergo additional surgery after reassessment at 36–60 hours if the eye worsened. This compared initial treatment strategies with additional care available.

Across the whole range of vision in this subgroup, the difference was significant (P < .001, log-rank). For hand motions or better presenting vision, routine immediate PPV showed no significant visual benefit. Adding the tested systemic antibiotics showed no significant improvement in final VA or media clarity.

Diabetes analysis · Arch Ophthalmol 2001

58 of 420 patients had diabetes. Diabetic and nondiabetic patients presenting with light perception only both did better with immediate vitrectomy. Among diabetic patients presenting better than light perception, 57% reached 20/40 with vitrectomy vs 40% with tap/biopsy. The difference wasn't statistically significant, and the authors called either approach reasonable for that group.

How far to trust it

Moderate for the light-perception result

Randomization and masked outcome measurement strengthen the study. But the key result comes from a subgroup that the paper doesn't describe as prespecified, and the reported values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Overall: some concerns. These are provisional appraisal judgments, not evidence that bias definitely occurred.

Presenting visual acuity changed the observed benefit of immediate surgery. That threshold rests on telling light perception from hand motions, the crudest vision test there is, done in a hazy, inflamed eye. Misjudge it by one step and the patient lands on the other side of the rule.

No significant difference in the hand-motions-or-better group does not formally establish equivalence. The factorial design also assumes that surgery and systemic antibiotics don't interact.

Findings directly concern acute postcataract and secondary-IOL endophthalmitis. I would avoid automatically extending them to endogenous, traumatic, fungal, or post-injection cases.

The systemic-antibiotic result concerns the specific regimen tested. Ceftazidime and amikacin penetrate the vitreous poorly, and newer oral agents such as moxifloxacin reach much better vitreous levels, so the null result says little about them.

The surgical technique reflects the study's era. EVS used 20-gauge vitrectomy; today's small-gauge surgery is quicker and safer, which is partly why many surgeons now operate earlier than EVS strictly requires.

Light-perception-only eyes reaching 20/40 or better at 9 months (55 vs 47 patients with final data): 22 percentage points more, a number needed to treat of about 5.

What I'd change

Standardize how presenting vision is measured and confirm the light perception / hand motions call with a second examiner, since the decision rests on the crudest vision test.

Clinical significance takeaway

On call, I'd check presenting vision carefully. Light perception only: immediate vitrectomy with intravitreal antibiotics (vancomycin 1 mg and amikacin 0.4 mg in EVS; most centers now use ceftazidime 2.25 mg instead of amikacin). Hand motions or better: tap and inject, reassess closely, and keep a low threshold for vitrectomy if the eye worsens, especially in diabetic patients. I would avoid extending this automatically to endogenous, traumatic, fungal or post-injection cases.

Bottom line

EVS 1995, for post-cataract endophthalmitis with LP-only vision, immediate PPV + intravitreal vancomycin 1 mg/amikacin 0.4 mg beats tap and inject (33% vs 11% reach 20/40); HM or better, tap and inject is enough, and IV antibiotics add nothing.
