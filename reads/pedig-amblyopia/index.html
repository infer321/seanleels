PEDIG amblyopia trials: patching or atropine

In moderate amblyopia, patching and atropine each improved vision by about 3 lines at 6 months in children aged 3 to under 7. Follow-up trials showed that 2 hours of patching worked as well as 6, weekend atropine as well as daily, and 6 hours as well as full-time patching in severe amblyopia.

Study snapshot

Design
ATS-1, the first Pediatric Eye Disease Investigator Group (PEDIG) amblyopia trial: multicenter randomized comparison of patching vs atropine at 47 clinical sites
Population
419 children aged 3 to under 7 with amblyopia and acuity 20/40 to 20/100
Treatment
Patching of the sound eye vs atropine eyedrops in the sound eye. The patching and atropine regimens used in ATS-1 were not verified from the abstract
Primary outcome
Visual acuity in the amblyopic eye at 6 months
Follow-up trials
Each randomized 2 regimens of the same treatment (patching hours, atropine frequency, or patching vs atropine in older children), using the same PEDIG network
Funding
US public health service research support (listed in PubMed); sponsor details not verified

Read with

  • Follow-upRepka et al., Arch Ophthalmol 2008 (age 10 years)The improvement from ATS-1 held to age 10. Residual amblyopia was common, and the two original treatments did not differ.
  • Same programHolmes et al., J AAPOS 2004An observational follow-up: about 1 in 4 successfully treated children relapsed within a year, and relapse was more common when 6-hour patching was stopped abruptly rather than reduced first.
  • Same programScheiman et al., Arch Ophthalmol 2005Ages 7 to 17: treatment still helped most 7- to 12-year-olds, and teens only if they had not been treated before.

Effect

Both treatments worked, and the gap between them at 6 months was small and clinically inconsequential. Patching improved vision faster at first. In ATS-1, more atropine-treated children had reduced sound-eye acuity at 6 months, but this did not persist with further follow-up, and parents found atropine slightly more acceptable. The strongest message of the later trials is that more intense treatment was not better: lighter regimens matched heavier ones.

What this was not

ATS-1 did not test 2 hours of patching against weekend atropine in young children. Those regimens came from later trials, and the head-to-head in older children was a separate study of ages 7 to 12. Improvement is not cure: at age 10, about 46% of treated eyes were 20/25 or better and the mean was about 20/32, so many children are left with some residual amblyopia. The 2-hour patching trial prescribed near activities in both groups, so it cannot show that those activities caused the benefit.

Who it applies to

These are children with strabismic or anisometropic amblyopia, mostly 3 to under 7 years old. The results should not be extended automatically to deprivation amblyopia, very young infants, or teenagers who have already been treated.

How far to trust it

High that patching and atropine both help; moderate for the exact regimen cut-offs

The finding is consistent across ATS-1, its 10-year follow-up and several later randomized trials from the same network, with objective acuity outcomes. I hold the regimen details more loosely: each follow-up trial was a 4-month comparison in a specific acuity range, and families could not be masked to the treatment.

What I'd change

Report acuity at a fixed long-term time point for every randomized child, not only for a subgroup who agreed to later follow-up. Measure adherence objectively (for example with a patch sensor) instead of by parental report. Test treatments in teens and in deprivation amblyopia, and compare regimens on stereoacuity and quality of life, not acuity alone.

Clinical significance takeaway

Correct refractive error first, then pick a regimen the family can sustain, because more intense treatment was not better. For moderate amblyopia in young children, 2 hours of daily patching or weekend atropine is a reasonable start. For severe amblyopia, 6 hours of patching matched full-time. Recheck acuity in both eyes to catch reverse amblyopia, assess adherence before escalating, and expect that some children keep a residual deficit and that about 1 in 4 may relapse after stopping, so reduce treatment before stopping rather than abruptly.

Bottom line

PEDIG ATS-1 2002: for moderate amblyopia in children aged 3 to under 7, patching and atropine improved vision about 3 lines at 6 months (3.16 vs 2.84), and later trials showed 2 hours of patching or weekend atropine is enough; fix refractive error first, otherwise use 6 hours of patching for severe cases.
