    Calibrate your screen first
    
          
    Hold a credit card or driver’s license against the screen with its short edge along the bar. Drag until the bar is exactly as long as the card is tall (54 mm). Everything below uses this to draw true-size targets.
    
          
    
            
    
          
    
          
    
            
            
            
          
    
          
        
    

        
    
          
    Near card
    
          
    Hold the screen at the test distance. Cover one eye. The patient points the way the legs of each E point. Record the smallest line where they get more than half right, with readers or a pinhole if they have them. Tap a line to reshuffle it.
    
          
    
            
    
              
              
            
    
            
            
          
    
          
    
          
    A screening aid, not a validated acuity chart. Use a printed card when one is available. Lines marked in grey are near the limit of what your screen can draw sharply.
    
        
    

        
    
          
    Pupil gauge
    
          
    Hold beside the patient’s eye and match each pupil to the nearest circle. Measure in bright light and again in a dim room when the sizes differ.
    
          
    
        
    

        
    
          
    Red desaturation
    
          
    Show the red disc to each eye in turn. Ask whether the red looks equally bright. If one eye sees it washed out or pinkish, ask how red it looks compared with the good eye (e.g. “60%”). Asymmetry suggests optic nerve disease.
    
          
    
        
    

        
    
          
    OKN strip
    
          
    Moving stripes trigger optokinetic nystagmus, a reflex that needs some working vision. If a patient reports no light perception but their eyes track the stripes, some vision is present. Hold it about 50 cm away and watch the eyes.
    
          
    
            
            
    
              
              
            
    
            
    
              
              
              
            
    
            
          
    
          
    
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    
        
    
          
    Don’t, before ophtho sees them
    
          
      
            
    • Don’t dilate.The pupil exam is a neuro vital sign, and dilating drops can trigger angle closure. Ask first.
      • 
            
    • Don’t patch a possible open globe.Use a rigid shield that rests on bone. Don’t patch a contact lens wearer’s abrasion either; it raises infection risk.
      • 
            
    • Don’t press on an injured eye.No forcing swollen lids open, no tonometry, and no removing impaled objects if an open globe is possible.
      • 
            
    • Don’t send topical anesthetic home.Repeated use melts the cornea.
      • 
            
    • Don’t start steroid drops blind.Without a slit lamp exam, they can worsen herpes or fungal keratitis and raise eye pressure.
      • 
            
    • Don’t wait on ophtho to start the clock-sensitive work.Stroke workup for a suspected CRAO and irrigation for a chemical injury start before the page is answered.
      • 
          
    
        
    

        
    
          
    Usually fine for clinic follow-up
    
          
    When vision is normal and there is no pain, photophobia, or contact lens use:
    
          
      
            
    • Viral or allergic conjunctivitis
      • 
            
    • Subconjunctival hemorrhage without trauma (check BP, and INR if on warfarin)
      • 
            
    • Stye or chalazion
      • 
            
    • Chronic, stable vision loss
      • 
            
    • Dry eye
      • 
            
    • Diabetic retinopathy screening in a stable patient
      • 
          
    
          
    New flashes and floaters are the exception: they still need a dilated exam within days, even when everything else looks fine.
    
        
    

        
    
          
    Speak the language
    
          
    
            
    TermMeaning
    
            
              
    OD / OS / OURight eye / left eye / both eyes
    
              
    cc / sc / PHWith correction / without / through a pinhole
    
              
    CF / HMCounts fingers / sees hand motion
    
              
    LP / NLPLight perception / no light perception
    
              
    RAPDRelative afferent pupillary defect, found with the swinging flashlight test
    
              
    IOPIntraocular pressure, normally about 10–21 mmHg
    
              
    EOMExtraocular movements
    
              
    CVFConfrontation visual fields
    
              
    ACAnterior chamber, the space between cornea and iris
    
              
    SLE / DFESlit lamp exam / dilated fundus exam
    
                
          
    
        
    

        
    
          
    Eye drop cap colors
    
          
    Topical eye drops follow a standard cap color code, which helps identify what a patient is using when they can’t name it.
    
          
    
            
    RedDilating drops (mydriatics, cycloplegics)
    
            
    PinkSteroids
    
            
    TanAnti-infectives
    
            
    GrayNSAIDs
    
            
    Dark greenMiotics (pilocarpine)
    
            
    YellowBeta-blockers
    
            
    Dark blueBeta-blocker combinations
    
            
    PurpleAdrenergic agonists
    
            
    OrangeCarbonic anhydrase inhibitors
    
            
    TurquoiseProstaglandin analogues
    
          
    
          
    A red cap in a neuro-check patient’s bag is worth asking about: it may explain a dilated pupil.
